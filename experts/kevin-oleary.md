# Kevin O'Leary

> A summary of this person's public teachings, written in our own words. It is not written or endorsed by them. Check the sources before you quote anything.

## Who they are

Kevin O'Leary is a Canadian businessman, born in Montreal in 1954. He co-founded the software company SoftKey, which later took the name The Learning Company and was sold to Mattel in 1999 for about $4.2 billion. He has been an investor on the TV show Shark Tank since 2009 (where he is called "Mr. Wonderful"), was on Canada's Dragons' Den from 2006 to 2014, and was chairman of O'Shares, an exchange traded fund (ETF) brand whose funds moved to SS&C ALPS Advisors in 2022. In 2025 and 2026 he also became known for large data center and energy projects, such as the Stratos Project in Utah. People listen to him for one thing above all: a hard, numbers first look at whether a business makes money.

## Use this expert when

- You want to know if your business actually makes money on each sale (unit economics)
- You need to check your margins, cash flow or prices
- You are about to pitch an investor, a lender or a partner
- You are deciding how much of your cash to bet on one thing
- You want a cold, honest test of whether a project is a business or a hobby
- You are planning personal money habits as a founder (saving, investing, debt)

## Core frameworks

### The three part pitch

- **What it is:** a simple test for any pitch. Can you explain the idea fast, show why you are the right person, and give your numbers?
- **How to use it:**
  1. Say what the business does in about 90 seconds or less.
  2. Say why you (or your team) can make it work. Past experience counts.
  3. Give the key numbers from memory: sales, margins, cost to get a customer.
  4. If you cannot do step 3, stop and learn your numbers before you pitch.
- **Source:** Soapcentral write up of his interview (see Sources).

### Know your numbers

- **What it is:** he will not invest if you do not know the basic math of your business.
- **How to use it:** have these ready at all times:
  1. Gross margin (our addition, not from his cited talks: what is left from each sale after the direct cost of making or delivering it)
  2. Customer acquisition cost, or CAC (what you spend in ads and sales to win one customer)
  3. Lifetime value, or LTV (how much one customer pays you over the whole time they stay)
  4. Make sure CAC is lower than LTV. If it is not, every new customer loses you money.
- **Source:** MIT Sloan talk, Modern Retail interview

### The royalty deal

- **What it is:** instead of only buying a piece of the company, he asks for a small payment on every unit sold (a royalty), often until he has his money back, sometimes forever.
- **How to use it:**
  1. Think like an investor who wants cash back soon, not just a big payout someday.
  2. Ask: if someone gave us money, how fast could our sales pay it back?
  3. Use this to test your own cash flow, even if you never take an investor.
- **Source:** FinanceBuzz on his Shark Tank deals, BNN Bloomberg interview, Modern Retail (Blueland deal)

### The 5 and 20 rule (diversification)

- **What it is:** a personal investing rule he credits to his mother. No more than 5% of your money in any one stock, and no more than 20% in any one sector (type of industry).
- **How to use it:**
  1. List where your money sits.
  2. If one stock grows past 5%, sell some to bring it back down.
  3. If one sector passes 20%, rebalance (move money around to get back to your plan).
- **Source:** Benzinga write up (credit to his mother), BNN Bloomberg interview, Moneywise

### The three year rule

- **What it is:** if a startup cannot make money after about three years and has no clear path to profit, he says it is a hobby, not a business.
- **How to use it:**
  1. Write down when the business or project started.
  2. Check if it is making a profit, or has a real, dated plan to get there.
  3. If not, decide on purpose: change it, or shut it down.
- **Source:** Entrepreneur, "Kevin O'Leary's 7 golden tips for startups"

## Key principles

- Sales come first. In the first two years, focus on selling, because sales prove the idea and bring in cash.
- Cash flow matters more than a big future story.
- A business that cannot make money is a hobby.
- Never put everything on one bet.
- Sell some of your winners when they get too big a part of your money.
- Cut your losses and do not dwell on them.
- Do not let taxes alone decide when you sell. Paying tax on a gain means you made money.
- Most new brands fail, so plan for losers and let a few winners carry you.
- Trust your gut. He says his worst deal came from ignoring a bad feeling.

## Mindset

- He runs his life like a portfolio: many things going at once, so one bad day is not the end.
- He keeps moving forward through good and bad news and tries not to get too high or too low.
- He likes founders who have failed before, because they learned from it.
- He is blunt on purpose. The goal is to show you the hard truth before the market does.

## Strategies and plays

- **Sell online before stores.** He says stores take a big cut of margin and tie up cash in stock. Example: start with your own site and online sales, then add stores later.
- **Test, then pour fuel on what works.** Try several sales channels, cut the weak ones, and spend more on the winner. Example: run 3 small ad tests, keep the one with the lowest CAC.
- **Own your customer.** He likes brands that sell about half direct to customers, for feedback and control. Example: collect emails from every buyer.
- **Give a discount for cash up front.** Early on, offer a discount to customers who pay in advance. Example: 10% off for paying for 3 months now.
- **Trim winners.** When one investment grows too big, sell part of it. Example: he has said he sold Tesla shares when they passed 5% of his holdings.
- **Save a set share of every paycheck.** He has told young people to save 20% of every paycheck. Example: set up an automatic transfer on payday.
- **Get out of debt.** He wants people debt free, mortgage included, by about age 45.

## Tools they use or recommend

He does not name many software tools. What he has named in public:

- **ETFs and index funds** for most people, instead of picking single bonds. Source: BNN Bloomberg interview.
- **Amazon** as a main sales channel for his portfolio brands. Source: Modern Retail interview.
- **Shark Tank itself** as a way to cut customer acquisition cost, since it reaches millions of viewers. Source: MIT Sloan talk.
- **His books:** *Cold Hard Truth: On Business, Money and Life* (2011), *Cold Hard Truth on Men, Women and Money* (2012), *Cold Hard Truth on Family, Kids and Money* (2015). Source: Wikipedia, Penguin Random House.
- **Simple tools you can build from his rules:** a one page numbers sheet (margin, CAC, LTV, cash), and a holdings list checked against the 5 and 20 rule.

Note: he is tied to the O'Shares ETF brand, so his ETF advice is not neutral.

## Questions they would ask you

Use these to pressure test a plan.

1. What is your gross margin on each sale?
2. How much does it cost you to get one customer, and how much do they pay you over time?
3. How long until this makes a profit? If the answer is "I don't know," why should anyone fund it?
4. Can you explain the whole business to me in 90 seconds?
5. Why are you the right person to run this?
6. If I gave you money today, how and when do I get it back?
7. What is your valuation based on, sales or hope?
8. How many months of cash do you have left?
9. What happens to you if this one thing fails? What else are you counting on?

## Watch out

- **He is an investor, not your customer.** His lens is about getting money back. Many good small businesses will never fit what a TV investor wants, and that is fine.
- **Royalty deals can hurt the founder.** A per unit royalty takes cash every sale, even when profit is thin. Some founders push back on them, so do the math before you agree to one.
- **The TV version is not the full picture.** What you see on the show is a short, cut down version of each pitch. The blunt style works on TV but can push people away in real sales and team talks.
- **The 5 and 20 rule is for investing, not your business.** A founder often has most of their money in their own company. That breaks the rule by design. Know the risk.
- **Some think the rules are too careful** for young investors with many years ahead.
- **He has business ties to products he talks about** (for example, his ETF company), so check advice that points to his own products.
- **Numbers are not everything.** He also says to trust your gut and back good people. Do not use him as an excuse to ignore customers or your team.
- **His past is mixed.** The Mattel deal is widely seen as a failed purchase for Mattel. Learn from his rules, not from the legend.
